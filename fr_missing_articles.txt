=== INDEX 12 ===
<p>The <strong>fire triangle</strong> is the fundamental principle of fire science. Fire requires three elements, and removing any one will extinguish the fire:</p><p><strong>Air (Oxygen):</strong> Fire needs oxygen to burn. This is why you build fire lays with gaps for airflow. Smothering a fire deprives it of oxygen — this is how a fire blanket or sand extinguishes flames. Too much wind can also blow out a small fire before it establishes.</p><p><strong>Heat:</strong> Heat is the energy that sustains the chemical reaction of combustion. Your spark from flint and steel provides initial heat. Once burning, the fire generates its own heat. Remove heat (with water, which cools) and the fire dies.</p><p><strong>Fuel:</strong> Fuel is what burns. In survival, this means wood in progressively larger sizes. Remove the fuel and the fire goes out — this is why you clear the area around your fire ring of combustible material.</p><p>Understanding the triangle helps you both build fires (provide all three) and extinguish them (remove one or more). Water removes heat and oxygen. Stirring exposes all hot spots. Earth smothers oxygen. A properly built fire lay ensures adequate airflow to all fuel.</p>

=== INDEX 13 ===
<p>The <strong>rule of three</strong> is a simple mnemonic that establishes survival priorities based on how long you can survive without each need:</p><p><strong>3 minutes without air:</strong> Oxygen is the most immediate need. In drowning, suffocation, or blocked airway situations, you have approximately 3 minutes.</p><p><strong>3 hours without shelter:</strong> In extreme conditions (freezing cold, intense heat, storm), you can survive about 3 hours without adequate shelter. This makes shelter your highest physical priority after air.</p><p><strong>3 days without water:</strong> Dehydration becomes critical after 3 days. Water sourcing and purification must happen early in your survival timeline.</p><p><strong>3 weeks without food:</strong> You can survive approximately 3 weeks without food. This makes food the lowest survival priority — you have time to figure it out after shelter, water, and fire are secured.</p><p>This rule directly informs the five elements of survival priority: Attitude, Shelter, Water, Fire, Food. Your mental attitude underpins everything, shelter addresses the 3-hour limit, water the 3-day limit, and food the 3-week limit.</p>

=== INDEX 14 ===
<p>The <strong>seven enemies of survival</strong> are the physical and psychological forces that erode your ability to survive. Each must be recognized and actively countered:</p><p><strong>Pain:</strong> Injuries cause pain that can overwhelm your judgment. Treat injuries with first aid, immobilize sprains, and manage pain so you can think clearly and act effectively.</p><p><strong>Cold:</strong> Hypothermia is the number one wilderness killer. Counter cold with shelter, fire, dry clothing, and insulation from the ground. Never let yourself stay wet and exposed.</p><p><strong>Thirst:</strong> You can survive only 3 days without water. Locate and purify water early. Ration sweat, not water — drink when you have it.</p><p><strong>Hunger:</strong> You can survive 3 weeks without food, so it is the lowest priority. But hunger saps energy and morale. Forage safely only when you can identify plants with 100% certainty.</p><p><strong>Fatigue:</strong> Exhaustion leads to mistakes and injuries. Schedule rest, pace your activities, and sleep when safe. A rested survivor makes better decisions.</p><p><strong>Boredom:</strong> Idle time in survival can be psychologically devastating. Stay busy with camp improvements, fire tending, signaling preparation, and routine tasks.</p><p><strong>Loneliness:</strong> Isolation erodes the will to live. Maintain hope, talk to yourself, keep a journal, and set daily goals. Your mental attitude is the most important survival tool you have.</p>

=== INDEX 15 ===
<p>The <strong>universal distress signal</strong> is <strong>three of anything</strong>. This pattern is internationally recognized:</p><p><strong>Three whistle blasts:</strong> Blow three sharp blasts on your survival whistle, pause, then repeat. A whistle carries much farther than a voice and uses less energy.</p><p><strong>Three mirror flashes:</strong> Flash a signal mirror three times toward aircraft or distant observers. The mirror flash can be seen for many kilometres on a sunny day.</p><p><strong>Three fires:</strong> Three fires arranged in a <strong>triangle</strong>, spaced <strong>35 metres apart</strong>, is an internationally recognized aerial distress signal. This is clearly distinguishable from a single campfire or natural fire.</p><p><strong>Three gunshots:</strong> If you have a firearm, three shots in succession signals distress. (Use only if safe and legal.)</p><p><strong>Three piles of smoke:</strong> Three separate smoky fires (add green branches to produce white smoke) are visible from the air during daylight.</p><p><strong>Ground-to-air signal symbols:</strong> Mark symbols on open ground using rocks, logs, or trenches. Each symbol should be <strong>1 metre wide and 8 metres long</strong>, with symbols placed <strong>3 metres apart</strong>. Key symbols: <strong>V</strong> (require assistance), <strong>X</strong> (require medical assistance), <strong>F</strong> (need food and water), <strong>arrow</strong> (proceeding in this direction).</p>

=== INDEX 16 ===
<p>The most critical survival tool is not a knife, fire, or shelter — it is <strong>your mind</strong>. Survival is 90% psychology. The cadets who survive are those who maintain a positive, determined attitude.</p><p><strong>Panic is the first enemy.</strong> When disaster strikes, the natural response is panic. Panic causes irrational decisions, aimless running, and wasted energy. The S.T.O.P. procedure exists specifically to combat panic — sitting down forces you to stop, breathe, and think.</p><p><strong>The REACT acronym</strong> helps manage psychological stress:</p><p><strong>R</strong> — Recognize the situation. Do not deny reality. Accept that you are in a survival situation.</p><p><strong>E</strong> — Expect to survive. Believe you will make it. This belief drives all your actions.</p><p><strong>A</strong> — Assess your resources. What do you have? What can you use? What are your priorities?</p><p><strong>C</strong> — Create a plan. Use S.T.O.P. Make decisions based on your assessment and the rule of three.</p><p><strong>T</strong> — Take action. Execute your plan. Staying busy fights boredom and gives you purpose.</p><p><strong>Counter the psychological enemies:</strong> Boredom and loneliness can destroy your will to survive. Fight them by setting daily goals, maintaining routines, keeping your camp organized, tending your fire, and keeping hope alive. Talk to yourself, sing, or keep a log. Your mind is your best friend or your worst enemy in survival — choose to make it your ally.</p>

=== INDEX 17 ===
<p>Water is your third survival priority. Here are methods to locate water in the wild:</p><p><strong>Animal signs:</strong> Animal trails often lead to water. Follow them downhill, especially if multiple trails converge. Grazing animals typically drink at dawn and dusk.</p><p><strong>Bird flight patterns:</strong> Birds, especially waterfowl, often fly toward water at dawn and away at dusk. Follow their flight direction at these times.</p><p><strong>Vegetation:</strong> Lush, green vegetation in an otherwise dry area indicates water nearby. Willows, cottonwoods, and cattails grow near water. Dig near their roots in dry stream beds — water may be just below the surface.</p><p><strong>Natural drainage:</strong> Follow ravines, gullies, and valley floors downhill. Water flows to the lowest point. Dry stream beds may have water beneath the surface — dig in the lowest outside bend.</p><p><strong>Dew collection:</strong> In the absence of flowing water, collect morning dew by wiping grass and leaves with a clean cloth, then wringing it into a container. This can provide significant water in dew-heavy areas.</p><p><strong>Snow and ice:</strong> Melt snow or ice for water. Prefer ice over snow — it yields more water per volume. Always melt before drinking to avoid lowering your core temperature.</p><p><strong>Beware:</strong> Avoid water with unusual color, odor, or foam. Avoid water downstream of mines, settlements, or agricultural areas. And always purify before drinking.</p>

=== INDEX 18 ===
<p>When wood is wet from rain, snow, or high humidity, finding dry tinder and kindling is challenging. <strong>Feather sticks</strong> are the solution.</p><p><strong>What is a feather stick?</strong> A feather stick is a split piece of wood with many thin shavings (feathers) left attached to the stick. The thin shavings catch fire easily, and the dry inner wood of the stick sustains the flame.</p><p><strong>How to make feather sticks:</strong></p><p>1. Find a dry-standing dead branch (still attached to the tree, not on the ground where it absorbs moisture). Standing dead wood is drier than fallen wood.</p><p>2. Split the branch open with your knife or axe. The inner wood is protected from moisture and should be dry even when the outside is wet.</p><p>3. Hold the split stick firmly. With your knife, make downward shaving strokes, leaving each shaving attached to the stick at the bottom. Make many thin shavings — 20 or more per stick.</p><p>4. The shavings should curl outward like feathers. The thinner and more numerous the curls, the easier they will catch a spark.</p><p>5. Make several feather sticks. The first catches the spark, and additional sticks provide progressively more fuel as the fire grows.</p><p>Feather sticks are a critical survival skill because wet conditions are when you most need a fire (for warmth) and when fire is hardest to start. Master this technique and you can light a fire in almost any conditions.</p>
